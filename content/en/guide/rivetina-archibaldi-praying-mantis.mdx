## What Is the Rivetina archibaldi Praying Mantis?

*Rivetina archibaldi* is a praying mantis in the genus *Rivetina*, family Rivetinidae, order Mantodea. It was described from the Zagros oak forests of Iran and published as a short communication in the [Journal of Orthoptera Research](https://jor.pensoft.net/article/174839/) on 12 June 2026. The paper's title tells you the essentials: a new species, a clear family placement, and a habitat that is unusual in mantis literature — which more often spotlights deserts, steppes, and scrub.

Because the species is new to science, there is no deep archive of field notes, captive-breeding reports, or community observation records to draw on. What exists is the formal description itself: taxonomy, comparative morphology, locality data, and a male-based identification key covering the Iranian members of the genus. That is entirely normal for a freshly described insect. The biology usually follows years later, built up by whoever visits the type locality with a notebook and patience.

A quick note on the name. *Rivetina* is the genus; *archibaldi* is the species epithet. Epithets ending in *-i* conventionally honor a person, though the description itself is the place to confirm any dedication. There is no widely established common name yet, so most people simply refer to it as the Rivetina archibaldi praying mantis.

## Why the Zagros Oak Forests Matter

The Zagros range sweeps roughly 1,500 kilometers from northwest to southeast Iran, and its oak woodlands are among the most distinctive forest systems in the Middle East. They are not rainforests. Summers are hot and dry, winters are cold, and at higher elevations snow is part of the annual rhythm. Trees are dominated by oaks, with Brant's oak (*Quercus brantii*) shaping large areas of the lower and middle slopes.

For a mantis, that adds up to a short, intense growing season. Insects must hatch, hunt, mature, mate, and lay eggs within a narrow window of warmth and foliage. Ground-dwelling mantises in these landscapes tend to be cryptic, fast-moving, and tightly associated with particular microhabitats — bark, leaf litter, low shrubs, or the base of grass tufts.

That is why a forest record matters. When a new mantis turns up in oak woodland rather than open desert, it hints at how much microhabitat diversity exists under the canopy and how poorly that diversity has been sampled.

## How Rivetina archibaldi Fits Into the Mantis Family Tree

*Rivetinidae* is a family recognized in modern mantis classification; its members were historically lumped into a much broader *Mantidae*. The genus *Rivetina* is a group of largely ground-associated mantises distributed across dry country from southeastern Europe and North Africa through the Middle East into Central Asia. Many species are compact, cryptically colored, and more inclined to sprint into cover than to fly.

That contrasts sharply with the mantis most people know. The European mantis (*Mantis religiosa*) is a foliage hunter, often seen perched on tall grass and shrubs, and it has been introduced far beyond its native range. A Zagros oak forest specialist occupies a different niche entirely — lower, more cluttered, more dependent on ground-level structure.

## How a New Mantis Species Gets Described

Understanding the process helps you read the paper critically. Describing a species is not a matter of spotting something odd and giving it a name — it is a structured argument that a population is genuinely distinct from everything already named.

| Step | What researchers do |
|---|---|
| 1. Collect and record | Gather specimens with precise locality, date, and habitat notes |
| 2. Compare morphology | Examine body proportions, spines, wing venation, and male genitalia |
| 3. Check the literature | Compare against original descriptions and existing regional keys |
| 4. Test with molecular data | Sequence markers where resources allow |
| 5. Deposit types | Place name-bearing specimens in a permanent collection |
| 6. Register and publish | Register the name, then pass peer review |

The male-based key in the *Rivetina archibaldi* paper is worth highlighting. Keys built on males are standard practice in mantis taxonomy because male genital structures are among the most reliable diagnostic characters. The practical consequence for field observers is simple: identifying a female or a nymph with confidence is often difficult, and expert confirmation is the honest answer.

### Reading the paper like a researcher

- Start with the diagnosis, not the description — it tells you what separates the species from its closest relatives.
- Note the type locality carefully; it defines where future records should be compared.
- Check the measurements table for ranges, not single figures.
- Look at which characters are described as variable; those are the ones that will confuse you in the field.

## Observing the Rivetina archibaldi Praying Mantis Ethically

Field observation of a newly described species should be low-impact by default. Small-range insects can be locally abundant and still fragile, and the first few years after a description are exactly when baseline data matter most.

| Practice | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Search, don't strip | Check bark, litter, and shrub bases by eye rather than sifting habitat apart |
| Photograph before handling | A clear dorsal and lateral image is often enough for expert review |
| Record exact coordinates and date | Distribution data is the most valuable contribution amateurs can make |
| Release where found | Moving insects between sites muddies locality records |
| Skip collection without permits | Iran requires authorization for scientific collecting |
| Log observations on a platform | Citizen-science records become citable, verifiable data points |

Timing matters too. In seasonally dry oak woodland, mantis activity clusters around the warmest, greenest stretch of the year, and many species are easiest to find as maturing nymphs rather than as adults. A slow walk at the edge of oak stands, checking trunk bases and low shrubs in early morning, beats a fast midday sweep every time.

If you photograph something you think is a Rivetina archibaldi praying mantis, keep the images, note the habitat, and pass them to a mantis specialist or a regional natural history collection. Vague social media identifications are far less useful than a dated record with coordinates.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is the Rivetina archibaldi praying mantis dangerous to humans?**
No. Like nearly all mantises, it is a small insect that feeds on other invertebrates. A large individual might give a brief pinch if handled roughly, but it is not venomous and poses no medical risk.

**Where does the Rivetina archibaldi praying mantis live?**
It was described from the Zagros oak forests of Iran. Its known range is based on the specimens examined in the original description, so the true distribution may be broader or more restricted than currently documented.

**Can I keep one as a pet?**
It is not advisable. The species is newly described, its captive husbandry is undocumented, and collecting in Iran requires permits. Enthusiasts are better served keeping well-established captive-bred species and contributing field records instead.

**When was this species discovered?**
The formal description was published on 12 June 2026 in the Journal of Orthoptera Research. As with most new insects, specimens were collected before the paper appeared, so the discovery itself predates publication.

**How do I identify one?**
Start with the male-based key in the published description, then seek expert confirmation. Genus-level traits will get you close; species-level certainty usually requires a specimen in hand or a very clear photo series reviewed by a specialist.
