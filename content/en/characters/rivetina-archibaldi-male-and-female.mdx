## A Field Checklist for Sexing Live Mantises

Work through the checks in order. The first two resolve most specimens; the rest are tiebreakers.

1. **Build and mass.** A broad, heavy body with a wide abdomen is female-leaning. A narrow, almost cylindrical body is male-leaning.
2. **Wings versus abdomen tip.** Wings extending clearly past the abdomen point male; wings ending short of the tip point female.
3. **Head proportions.** Larger, rounder eyes and longer antennae lean male.
4. **Behavior and perch.** A mantis actively walking through open ground or arriving at a light leans male; one sitting tight on a low stem for long periods leans female.

| Check | Cost | Confidence | Best done |
|---|---|---|---|
| Body build and mass | Seconds | High | In hand or good macro photo |
| Wing length vs. abdomen | Seconds | Medium | Lateral photo |
| Antennae and eye proportions | A minute | Medium | Close-up head shot |
| Behavior over 5+ minutes | Minutes | Medium | Observation only |
| Ventral abdominal view | A minute | High | Specimen in hand |

For photographs, take three frames: a true dorsal shot, a lateral shot showing the wing tip against the abdomen, and a head-on close-up. Add a scale reference — a coin, a ruler segment, or a grid card. Photos without scale lose half their value for later comparison, and dorsal-only shots routinely hide the wing-tip character entirely.

One more caution: nymphs of both sexes look alike through the early instars. Any "female" identified from a penultimate-instar nymph is a guess until the final molt.

That behavioral split creates a predictable sampling bias. Males wander, so they turn up in traps and on the move far more often than females do. ## Common Misidentifications and How to Avoid Them

Most errors with **Rivetina archibaldi male and female** material fall into a handful of repeatable categories.

| Mistake | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sexing from a nymph | Both sexes similar before the final molt | Wait for the adult molt or rear it out |
| Calling a worn male female | Wing tips broken short of the abdomen | Inspect the wing margin for damage |
| Assuming a female is a different species | She fails the male-based key | Check locality and body form against males from the same site |
| Confusing genera | Other ground mantises share the same habitat | Compare pronotum shape and head proportions, not just color |
| Teneral adults | Freshly molted specimens look soft and oddly proportioned | Wait a few hours for sclerotization |

Color is the least dependable character in the whole exercise. Ground mantises vary individually, and a single site can produce a range of tones. Structure — wing length, pronotum proportions, eye shape — holds up far better than shade.

## Practical Tips for Observers and Keepers

- **Get permission first.** Collecting in Iran requires permits, and protected woodland status varies by area. Photograph-and-release is the low-risk option.
- **Record locality precisely.** Coordinates, elevation, and habitat description turn a photo into a usable record.
- **Photograph both sexes from the same site if you can.** Paired records are worth far more than isolated ones for anyone working out female characters.
- **Label uncertain IDs as uncertain.** "Cf." and "probable" are honest and useful; overconfident labels pollute datasets.
- **Log molts and dates.** If you rear a wild-caught nymph, note each molt. It is the cleanest way to confirm sex before adulthood.
- **Treat hobbyist claims as community reports**, not verified data, until they are backed by photos and locality details.

For anyone building a reference collection of images, a simple three-column log — date, sex call, and confidence level — will save hours later. Reviewers of regional records consistently say the same thing: the bottleneck is not photographs, it is photographs with no context.
