## How to Read a Mantis Description Without Getting Lost

Taxonomic prose has its own dialect, and the fastest way in is to learn maybe a dozen terms. A **holotype** is the single specimen that anchors the name forever; **paratypes** are the supporting cast. The **type locality** is where the holotype came from, and it is often the most consequential line in the whole paper for field biologists, because it tells you where to go looking.

Pay particular attention to the **differential comparison**. Good descriptions do not just describe the new species — they explain precisely how it differs from the closest named relatives, character by character. That section is where identifications live or die.

| Term | Plain-language meaning |
|---|---|
| Holotype | The reference specimen for the species name |
| Paratype | Additional specimens cited alongside the holotype |
| Type locality | The place the holotype was collected |
| Differential diagnosis | The explicit "how it differs from X and Y" comparison |
| Etymology | The explanation of the name, often a patronym honoring a person |
| Depository | The museum or collection where specimens are archived |
| LSID / DOI | Persistent identifiers that keep the record findable |

A practical habit: read the taxonomy section twice. The first pass gives you the shape of the argument; the second pass, with the key open beside it, turns characters into a checklist you can apply to a photograph or a preserved specimen.

## Field Notes and Community Reports from the Zagros

Formal literature will not tell you what the mantis is doing on a Tuesday in June. That is where **community reports** from naturalists, photographers, and regional insect groups fill in — cautiously. Observations shared by hobbyists and local recorders can flag seasonality, habitat associations, and color variation long before anyone publishes on them, but they are hypotheses, not data, until a specimen or a verified image backs them up.

The recurring theme in community reports about ground mantises from this region is simple: look down, not up. *Rivetina* are not canopy hunters. They work leaf litter, rocky slopes, and low herbaceous cover, where their mottled browns and grays do exactly what camouflage is supposed to do.

| Photo to take | Why it matters for identification |
|---|---|
| Full dorsal view | Overall proportions, wing length, body shape |
| Head and pronotum, side-on | Shape of the pronotum and eye configuration |
| Forelegs, close-up | Spine arrangement on femur and tibia |
| Wings, folded | Whether the individual is macropterous or short-winged |
| Ventral abdomen tip | Sex determination in the field |
| Habitat wide shot | Oak woodland structure, substrate, elevation context |

Label any such observation clearly as community-sourced. A photograph with a location and date is useful; a photograph with a confident species label and no supporting characters is a liability that propagates errors through occurrence databases.

## Open Questions and How You Can Contribute

As with most newly described mantises, the description answers "what is it" far more thoroughly than "how does it live." Expect open questions around diet, phenology, nymphal stages, and the true extent of the range beyond the oak forest belt. Those gaps are not flaws — they are the standard state of affairs for a species known from a small number of specimens.

If you want to push the literature forward, the highest-value contributions are unglamorous:

- **Voucher photography** with precise locality and date, deposited where reviewers can check it.
- **Specimen deposition** in a recognized collection rather than a personal freezer, so future revisers can examine material.
- **DNA-grade samples** where legal and ethical, since barcoding is increasingly how cryptic mantis species get resolved.
- **Careful citation** of the original description every time the name appears, so the nomenclatural record stays anchored.

Anyone compiling a bibliography of **Rivetina archibaldi research papers** in the next few years should expect it to stay short for a while. Short communications on new species are typically followed by slower, larger revisions — and those revisions are where the genus finally makes sense.
